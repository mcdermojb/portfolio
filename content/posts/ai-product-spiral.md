I recently came across a post by **Mark Cardillo** that struck a chord, particularly his observation that documentation is often the first head on the chopping block when corporate budgets face the "belt-tightening" phase. It is an extremely timely issue. As AI dominates the conversation, I see more and more budgets being slashed under the guise of automation. But there is a fundamental misunderstanding at play here: we are told AI will automate rote tasks, yet the cuts are hitting the **knowledge workers**—the very people who manage the nuances AI cannot grasp.

## A product is only as good as its map
Mark is right: documentation *is* the product experience inception. It is a user's first interaction. If that interaction is fractured, the brand is tarnished before a feature is used. 

But what happens when we remove the human writers? We are told AI will fill the gap. However, AI lacks the one thing Mark identifies as essential: **empathy**. AI cannot put itself in the shoes of a frustrated user encountering a product for the first time. It can generate text, but it cannot "craft" an experience. Knowledge workers are pivotal because they know the fail points; they know the right questions to ask. When you cut the writer, you aren't just cutting a "cost center"—you are cutting the gatekeeper of quality.

## The cycle of decline
When these cuts happen, a predictable and damaging pattern emerges:

1.  **Defensive Tracking:** The remaining doc teams, stretched thin, implement rigorous ways to track every minute of their work to justify their existence. This bureaucracy stifles the innovation and product improvements they should be focused on instead.
2.  **The Backlog Explosion:** As Mark mentions, backlogs grow. With each release the doc defect backlog compounds. 
3.  **The Developer Drain:** Every one of those doc bugs requires a writer to track down a developer for information. Those developers are already "under the gun," forced to use AI to meet their own productivity targets. 

Is it any wonder that costs eventually increase while product value plummets?

## The illusion of savings
The "belt-tightening" crowd argues that these cuts save money. I would argue the opposite. Customers spot these bugs immediately. To them, the documentation is the first place they look to solve a problem. When they find a mess, they don't just think the docs are bad. They think the product is in decline. 

They begin to wonder: *"Why not find another product that does the same thing but is actually easier to use?"* A support request is often just the final step before churn. The brand image fades.

## Reclaiming the craft
We must remember that documentation is not a supplement; it is an inseparable part of the product. If we lose the human element—the empathy required to structure content, we lose the customer. 

I’m curious about the specific pressures others are seeing in their industries:
* **How are your teams currently measuring the "invisible" ROI of high-quality human-led documentation compared to AI-generated drafts?**
* **What specific metrics are being used in your organization to justify the retention of knowledge workers during budget reviews?**

Documentation should be leveraged as a prodcut feature, not a neglected line item. If we treat it like an afterthought, our customers will eventually treat our products the same way.
