The Obvious Cost of the AI 'Belt-Tightening' Delusion

Why cutting technical documentation teams in the age of AI triggers a compounding cycle of declining product quality and customer churn.
April 30, 2026
Table of Contents
  • A Product Is Only as Good as Its Map
  • The Cycle of Decline
  • The Illusion of Savings
  • Reclaiming the Craft

I recently read a post by Mark Cardillo that hit home—specifically his observation that documentation is often the first head on the chopping block during corporate “belt-tightening.” As AI dominates executive conversations, budgets are increasingly slashed under the guise of automated efficiency. Yet there is a fundamental misunderstanding at play: while we are told AI will automate routine tasks, headcount reductions are targeting the knowledge workers who manage the nuances and edge cases AI cannot grasp.

A Product Is Only as Good as Its Map#

Documentation is the front door of the product experience. It is often a user’s first interaction with a platform, and if that entry point is broken, the brand is tarnished before a core feature is even evaluated.

When organizations eliminate human writers under the assumption that AI will fill the gap, they overlook a critical element: empathy. AI cannot anticipate the cognitive friction of a frustrated user navigating a system for the first time. It can generate syntactically correct text, but it cannot architect an intuitive user journey. Knowledge workers are pivotal because they spot systemic failure points and know which questions to ask. Cutting technical writers doesn’t eliminate a “cost center”—it removes the primary gatekeepers of product quality.

The Cycle of Decline#

Slashing documentation headcount triggers a predictable, destructive pattern:

  1. Defensive Bureaucracy: Overextended documentation teams implement rigid tracking overhead to quantify every minute of work, consuming time that should be spent improving content.
  2. Compounding Backlogs: Documentation defect backlogs compound with every release, progressively degrading content accuracy.
  3. Developer Friction: Resolving documentation bugs requires writers to interrupt engineering teams who are already under pressure to meet tight deadlines using uncalibrated AI tools.

Rather than lowering operating expenses, this cycle inflates internal friction while eroding product value.

The Illusion of Savings#

The assumption that cutting documentation saves capital is a delusion. Customers encounter documentation defects immediately—to them, documentation is the product. When users encounter incomplete or inaccurate guidance, they perceive product degradation rather than bad writing.

Poor documentation drives users directly to alternative solutions. A support ticket is rarely just a help request; it is often the final milestone before customer churn.

Reclaiming the Craft#

Documentation is not an auxiliary supplement—it is an integral product feature. Removing the human empathy required to structure complex information inevitably drives users away.

How is your organization navigating this shift?

  • Measuring ROI: How are your teams evaluating the ROI of human-crafted technical documentation versus AI-generated drafts?
  • Justifying Headcount: What specific metrics or frameworks are you using during budget reviews to justify retaining specialized knowledge workers?

If leadership treats documentation as an administrative afterthought, customers will eventually treat the product the same way.
